On the Information Set of Individual Investors* 
 
 
 
 
Toomas Laarits

Abstract 
 
Browser data from individual investors offers direct and detailed evidence about the information 
that shapes their beliefs. The median individual investor spends approximately six minutes on 
online research per trade on traded tickers, typically just before the trade, while the mean 
investor spends around thirty minutes. On average, individual investors allocate most research 
time to price charts, followed by analyst opinions, and do not show much interest in traditional 
portfolio or risk statistics. Information-gathering behavior is also heterogeneous: investors differ 
substantially in the depth and breadth of stock research, and those who trade speculative stocks 
are more likely to focus on short-term information.  
 
 
 
Keywords:  Individual investors; Clickstream data; Investor attention; Information acquisition; 
Household finance

I.  
Introduction 
Finance research often makes use of a stylized model of how individual investors trade. 
First, investors acquire information, developing an information set. Second, they process this 
information into beliefs. Third, they consider these beliefs in light of their preferences. Fourth, 
based on this consideration, they trade and/or form portfolios. Each stage of the process is 
complex, nuanced, and crucial to the next process. 
The stage about which least is known might be the one that sets the process in motion: 
what information investors actually gather. It is usually not possible to observe much detail about 
this process at a private, micro level, so one must often make assumptions about the information 
investors use to form beliefs. Such assumptions are diverse. Classic approaches presume interest 
in variances and covariances (Markowitz (1952) and Sharpe (1964)). Noise trader models like 
De Long, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann (1990) assume that some investors act on 
fundamentals while others form beliefs based on irrelevant information. In Hong and Stein 
(1999), some investors follow “news” while others react to price patterns. Barberis, Shleifer, and 
Vishny (1998) model beliefs based on attention to earnings realizations. Dividend signaling 
models posit that investors observe and interpret dividend payments. Several models emphasize 
attention to past market returns and return extrapolation (Barberis, Greenwood, Jin, and Shleifer 
(2015) and Adam, Marcet, and Beutel (2017)). Microstructure models and limited attention 
approaches consider still other information setups. While the amount and types of information 
investors gather often cannot be based on direct observation, the information structure is often 
essential to the meaning of these models.  
Information set concepts are also central to empirical research. Generations of finance 
students and scholars have thought about market efficiency through information sets that include 

=== PDF PAGE 3 ===
 
3
past prices only (weak form efficiency), all publicly available information (semi-strong form 
efficiency), and all public and private information (strong form efficiency). Event studies are 
interpreted in terms of the “market’s” information set that the results support.   
In this paper, we offer a more comprehensive empirical picture of the information that 
individual investors acquire. This allows us to treat the information set as an object as opposed to 
a disparate set of individual pieces. The results are based on a large sample of browser data from 
an approximately representative sample of U.S. individual investors. Timestamped URLs allow 
us to observe—or, in some cases, reconstruct using the Internet Archive—the range of 
investment-relevant stock research that occurs between and around an investor’s trades. To our 
knowledge, the first paper that uses a sample of individual investor clickstream data is Gargano 
and Rossi (2018). We build substantially on their findings, as their data are based on customers 
of a single broker and relative to our data, only coarser aspects of research activity within that 
one domain can be observed. More recently, Kwan, Liu, and Matthies (2026) use a clickstream 
data set containing news article reading activity by institutional investors, focusing on how they 
shift attention between macroeconomic and individual stock news as a function of aggregate 
volatility. In short, relative to these samples, our data contain especially high resolution, allowing 
us to uncover several basic facts about individual investor stock research and the information that 
such investors acquire.  
Specifically, the raw data for the investors that we focus on—households that trade any 
individual U.S. stocks or ADRs in any online brokerage account over four months of 2007—
include over 8 million clicks and 60,000 hours of Internet use. We identify 484 household-
traders (“individual investors”) who make 2,911 trades in the sample. Altogether, they use at 
least 28 distinct investment-relevant domains, including 8 online brokerages, and also sometimes

4
consult the corporate websites of stocks that they research. We stress that layouts of popular 
research domains have changed little since 2007, and even the top domains used in stock 
research are largely the same, so the analysis remains relevant today. The URL text string allows 
us to trace this activity and understand their information-gathering practices in some detail.  
With these data in hand, we can provide a detailed picture of key aspects of the 
information-gathering process and ultimately the basic building blocks of an individual 
investor’s information set. The process is high-dimensional and can’t be boiled down to a 
particular hypothesis test. To keep the analysis organized, we characterize research behavior in 
four main respects: how much research individual investors do (depth), what topics they focus on 
(topics), how these aspects differ across stocks and investors (heterogeneity), and when investors 
do their research in the context of an actual trade (timing).  
With respect to depth, our best estimate is that the median individual investor spends six 
minutes per trade on research specifically on the ticker(s) traded. The mean individual investor 
spends 29 minutes per trade. Including research about stocks that are not traded within the 
sample, thus capturing a notion of information acquisition but with no immediate bearing on 
market activity, leads to a best estimate that the median investor spends 36 minutes per trade and 
the mean investor spends 144 minutes per trade. Many investors conduct research across a 
number of domains; Yahoo Finance is by far the most important non-broker domain. 
With respect to the topics of research interest, for most investors the plurality of research 
time is devoted to price charts and technical information. This effort contrasts with standard 
academic advice, and extensive empirical research, that such information is at best of marginal 
investment value. Suggesting the importance of defaults, when price charts are consulted, the 
lookback provided by the page is usually the only lookback horizon considered even when many 

=== PDF PAGE 5 ===
 
5
other lookback horizons are easily selected. Simple snapshot pages with a set of summary 
statistics, also including a price chart, are also frequently consulted; indeed, they mark the 
beginning and end of many investor research sessions. Among fundamentals-oriented 
information, analyst opinions are relatively frequently consulted. Another robust conclusion is 
that traditional risk statistics attract relatively little specific attention, standing in contrast to the 
normative emphasis on risk statistics in classical asset pricing theory. 
We find significant heterogeneity both in the stocks of most aggregate interest as well as 
research styles across investors. In an extension of Gargano and Rossi’s analysis of cross-stock 
differences in aggregate interest, the main determinant of attention is firm size. Volatility, 
including poor recent and momentum returns, and earnings announcement periods also lead to 
greater attention. The first principal component of research differences across investors is the 
level of research interest across all topics (depth); tying together dimensions of heterogeneity, the 
second shows that investors who give most attention to speculative stocks tend to focus on short-
term or fast-moving information, such as message board discussions and price charts, while 
those who focus on more mature and stable firms are likelier to consider fundamentals.  
Finally, with respect to timing, the median individual investor conducts the biggest burst 
of her stock-specific research in the minutes before a trade. She spends a similar total amount of 
time in wide windows around buy and sell trades, but attention ramps up more sharply prior to 
buys. To conclude the investigation, we trace typical investors’ paths through topics and stages 
of analysis that surround trades, which identifies the topics that attract initial research attention 
and the topics that are the last to be considered prior to an actual trade. 
The results contribute toward the unification of the large literature on investor attention, 
since clickstream data offer a direct measure of attention and information gathering. Barber and 

=== PDF PAGE 6 ===
 
6
Odean (2008) showed that stocks with heavy news coverage or extreme daily returns or volume 
capture the attention of retail investors, although the underlying topics generating the news, 
returns, and volume are not always clear; we are able to shed light on this. Tetlock (2010) studies 
the impact of news on return reversal and volume-induced momentum. Da, Engelberg, and Gao 
(2015) find that aggregate Google search volume about economic downturns predicts market 
volatility and fund flows. Subsequent work has sought to further disaggregate types of 
information commanding attention, such as Kwan et al. who study online news article accesses 
and relate them to portfolio allocations of institutional investors. Drake, Roulstone, and 
Thornock (2012) see an elevated level of Google search volume around earnings 
announcements. 
The attention literature has made much progress, but also presents some limitations that 
we can address here. One pointed out by many authors is that direct measures of attention are 
difficult to observe. Another is that the literature is a bit fragmented in that different papers 
identify particular elements of attention. If the aggregate information set of individual investors 
were a pot of alphabet soup, research has established the existence of certain letters somewhere 
in somebody’s soup, such letters being earnings announcements or recent returns. However, 
high-resolution micro data allow us to estimate the full distribution of letters in the aggregate pot, 
directly observe the distribution within 484 particular bowls, and also observe trading activity 
alongside attention. In this way, the analysis brings together some of the associations observed in 
the attention literature and their distributions and relative prominence. 
More generally, the stylized facts about depth, breadth, heterogeneity, and timing of 
research behavior help to guide asset pricing frameworks toward promising assumptions about 
the upstream information-gathering process, and thus toward more accurate information set 

=== PDF PAGE 7 ===
 
7
assumptions on which to build downstream beliefs, trades and portfolios, and prices. As one 
example, modeling approaches may need to incorporate a more explicit role for beliefs based on 
historical price patterns, perhaps at default lookback horizons provided by research sources, to 
get accurate predictions about individual investor behavior. Greenwood and Shleifer (2014) work 
in this direction. On the other hand, models that suppose individual investors make trading 
decisions based on considered judgments about fundamentals and risk characteristics are on 
shakier foundations. As another example, models that feature heterogeneity across investors, 
with different trading habitats working together with different research habits, may be more 
promising than representative investor approaches meant to fit the whole cross-section of traders 
and stocks. In the text, we use bullet points to highlight additional specific findings.  
Section II describes the data and the sample of investors. Section III gives basic statistics 
on total stock research. Section IV documents which categories of information are of most 
interest. Section V studies how investor research varies across stocks. Section VI explores 
heterogeneity in research approaches. Section VII studies research patterns in the context of a 
trade. Section VIII concludes and comments on directions that we leave to future work, such as 
connecting the details of information gathering to trading and performance in a more granular 
way than can be done in the absence of this detail. The Internet Appendix supplements our brief

II. 
Sample and methodology 
 
We begin by introducing the clickstream data structure, the main household (“individual 
investor”) sample, and the approach to identifying stock research.  
A. 
Clickstream data 

=== PDF PAGE 8 ===
 
8
 
Clickstream data are collected by several companies. Our data were made available by an 
online research company and were used in a legal context by Bakos, Marotta-Wurgler, and 
Trossen (2014) and Marotta-Wurgler (2011, 2012). The raw data contains the browsing behavior 
of tens of thousands of U.S. households in January, February, March, and June 2007, in the form 
of roughly two billion timestamped URLs. 
The data provider makes sophisticated efforts to reduce selection biases and form as 
representative a sample of U.S. households as practically possible. To paraphrase Bakos et al., 
the households in the sample were recruited using random-digit-dialing distributions. They 
installed a plug-in that collected the timing and sequence of URLs visited. All computers within 
the household had such data gathered and aggregated (coincidentally, the timing of the sample 
coincided with the announcement but not the widespread use of Apple’s iPhone). Confidential 
and/or personally identifiable data were removed by the data provider.  
To date, few studies in finance have had the opportunity to study clickstream data, and 
the relative advantages of our sample vis-à-vis those analyzed in prior work, such as Gargano 
and Rossi (2018) and Benamar, Foucault, and Vega (2021), are powerful. Our sample appears to 
be more representative of online households in the U.S., allowing for a greater degree of external 
validity; the data contain details at the URL level, allowing us to pursue a number of unique 
questions; and it includes all domains, as opposed to, e.g., a single broker, allowing us to provide 
a comprehensive account of an average individual investor’s activity.  
A preliminary is the extent to which our 2007 data sheds light on present practices. We 
suggest that it does. By 2007, online research and trading were already the norm (Bogan (2008)) 
and, with respect to research, the Internet was not just the easiest but often the only way to gather 
most of the stock-specific data items that we track. Consensus earnings, analyst updates, beta, 

=== PDF PAGE 9 ===
 
9
insider selling, institutional ownership, recent price technical patterns, forward P/E ratios—such 
metrics were, and are, easily accessible via free, continuously-updated data feeds which are a 
click or two away from the Buy and Sell buttons. Without Internet research, such data are at best 
cumbersome to obtain.  
To underscore how little has changed, Figure A1 compares some of the stock-level data 
detail available in Yahoo Finance in 2007 and today. Yahoo Finance is particularly relevant 
because it has been the most popular finance research domain for at least twenty years.1 Panel A 
shows what an investor who was interested in analyst estimates for Salesforce.com would have 
seen in our sample period, while Panel B shows what the investor would see as of this writing. 
The page layout is almost identical. The same information is presented, in the same order, with 
the same historical comparisons. The few differences are minor. In the recent page, some tabular 
information is also repeated in graphs, whereas in the older page, some additional detail about 
growth estimates was presented that is no longer available. We have found that the layouts of 
brokerage-domain research pages have also remained relatively static. In short, the sample 
appears to reasonably reflect today’s online environment. The Internet was, and remains, the 
obvious source for stock-specific information, and even the most-used domains have not 
changed.

While the data offer an account of the investor-level research process with both breadth 
and granularity, there are some caveats. By definition, the data do not indicate what individual 
investors learn or already knew from non-Internet sources. However, in a large number of 
situations, this is less of a concern than it may seem. Already as of 2007, the Internet was, and 
remains, the only convenient way for individual investors to gather almost all of the specific data 
items that we track.  
A subtler limitation is that the data do not indicate an investor’s accumulated “stock” of 
knowledge as opposed to the flow of information in the clickstream. For information that is 
short-lived, this is not an issue. An investor interested in a one-week-lookback price chart, for 
example, cannot make use of that information for long. For longer-lived information, such as 
dividend yields, it represents more of a limitation. To shed some light on this issue, we look into 
how research ramps up prior to the trade, reasoning that if a trader was relying on a reservoir of 
unobservable knowledge, we might not expect a burst of research on that stock prior to the trade. 
Such a pattern turns out to be fairly typical. 
Another qualification is that the time spent viewing a data item is not a perfect measure 
of its relevance to beliefs. Time spent may sometimes exaggerate, rather than understate, the 
effective scope of investor research, because we do not know how well investors actually 
understand the information presented to them. Conversely, spending only a few seconds viewing 
a bit of information does not always indicate a lack of understanding or a lack of interest. For 
instance, price charts can be grasped quickly. We therefore tabulate not just the average amount 
of time individual investors spend on a particular topic, but the percentage of investors that spend 
any time at all. If there is no attention at all to a data item that was a labeled hyperlink away, it 
was evidently not of much interest and did not enter the investor’s information set.

C. 
Defining stock research 
 
Learning about how individual investors distribute attention across underlying topics over 
a comprehensive set of potential information sources—eight brokers and over twenty non-broker 
websites/domains—is one of the most unique opportunities provided by the data.  
Whereas asset pricing theory and some empirical work often distinguishes between cash 
flow news and discount rate news, information is packaged differently in practice. Brokerage 
sites, for example, provide access to a broadly similar list of company-specific data items, and 
investors can then drill down into these within the same domain. Given a degree of heterogeneity 
in how websites present content and the detail available in URLs, we must choose an appropriate 

=== PDF PAGE 14 ===
 
14
level of granularity. If our topics are too granular, we risk misinterpreting patterns if an 
investor’s brokerage or preferred websites happen to offer slightly less-detailed information. If 
the topics are too coarse, we fail to use the full detail of the data. 
Balancing these considerations, and giving some deference toward topics emphasized in 
the literature, we identified the following as “stock research” and potentially essential aspects of 
the information set: (1) risk statistics, (2) earnings, (3) dividends, (4) other fundamentals 
(typically valuation ratios or financial statements), (5) analyst opinions, (6) informed ownership 
and trading, e.g., by insiders, funds, institutions, or short sellers, (7) price charts and price-related 
data, (8) technical signals, and (9) company website visits for research-relevant purposes. We 
also classify links as (10) “other” if the topic is determinable but of only rare interest and as (11) 
“indeterminate” if the link plainly constitutes stock research but lacks the detail to further 
categorize. Also included within our research totals are “snapshot” or quotes pages that present 
multiple topics at once; news items with insufficient detail to be classified further; and message 
board pages where the underlying content is not observable. We describe how we apportion such 
time to specific topics below and in the Internet Appendix.  
Two further points are worth noting. First, while we organize the discussion with 
reference to stock-specific information gathering, macroeconomic news also affects firm-level 
beliefs. Such research would usually be included as “indeterminate” in our classification. See 
Kwan et al. for a detailed study of institutional investor attention to macroeconomic versus 
stock-specific news that uses clickstream data. Second, we choose to focus on content, not 
presentation format. Essentially the same earnings information can be conveyed through a table, 
a chart, a news article, or a text press release, for example. While URLs often indicate the 
presentation format, and the manner in which a given unit of information is presented might 

=== PDF PAGE 15 ===
 
15
impact beliefs through salience effects, we leave that as a topic for future research. Here, we treat 
the content itself as the first-order input for forming beliefs and the natural starting point for 
academic study.  
 
III. 
Research depth: Total time spent 
 
With the sample of individual investors’ research behavior in hand, we start with some 
broad patterns. The first question, given comprehensive data including all brokers and finance-
related research for a roughly representative sample of individual investors, is simply how much 
online investment-related research they actually do. Standard theory and efficient markets 
research suggest it’s fruitless for an individual investor to spend much time reviewing public 
information. Investment advertising and the widespread availability of stock information on the 
Internet, on the other hand, suggests a different message. What are the facts?  
A. 
Total time (and main sources) 
 
Table 2 summarizes the total research activity conducted by our investors, summing up 
over all the topic categories and ticker symbols that were investigated. Panel A reports that 
investing households spend between one and four months in the sample, three months being the 
median. To maintain comparability between investors that spend different spells of time in the 
sample, we construct summary research measures as investor-level averages of monthly values, 
or normalize by the number of months in sample, or normalize by the number of trades by the 
investor, as indicated.  
Panel B provides summary statistics about overall research activity. Recall we define a 
“browsing session” as a sequence of clicks with a break no longer than fifteen minutes. The 
average investor carries out close to 29 such sessions a month that include at least some stock 

=== PDF PAGE 16 ===
 
16
research component, and in the course of these research sessions the average investor sees 31 
unique tickers and visits 3.3 distinct brokerage or other finance sites. We construct these count 
measures on the investor-month level, average to the household level, and report the statistics of 
the resulting distribution of N=484 values.  
In terms of research frequency, the median investor conducts about 15 research sessions 
per month and encounters 13 unique tickers over that time. The average browsing session with a 
research component includes 3.6 minutes of stock research. Over the course of the month, the 
average household carries out about 118 minutes of research, with a median at 37.1 minutes and 
the 95th percentile clocking in at close to nine hours per month (530 minutes). This heterogeneity 
(skew) across investors in research time gives more color to the heterogeneity in brokerage login 
rates across customers in Sicherman, Loewenstein, Seppi, and Utkus (2016). 
 
 We also calculate total research per trade. This measure sheds light on a basic Hayekian 
question: how much incremental information could be reflected in a typical trade by a typical 
individual investor. The full sample provides our best estimates of total research time per trade, 
including research on both untraded and traded tickers (we will break this down further in the 
next panel). The mean investor carries out 144.2 minutes of research per trade with the 95th 
percentile coming in at eleven hours, while the 25th and 50th percentile are at 10.5 and 36.4 
minutes, respectively.  
The visibility into domains also allows us to establish which sites are most commonly 
used. This shows the concentration of investor attention on individual websites, which in turn 
governs the structure of information most easily accessible to individual investors. While 
intellectual property interests of the data provider limit our ability to unmask most domains, we 
can establish some key facts. One of the most noteworthy is that information gathering typically 

=== PDF PAGE 17 ===
 
17
goes beyond the brokerage’s domain. Specifically, the mean (median) investor spends 55.7 
(13.3) minutes per trade at the broker’s site, 45.3 (0.1) minutes at Yahoo Finance, and 41.9 (3.5) 
minutes on one or more of the other twenty-odd finance-relevant sites that they use. For the 
research-intensive investors that most affect the mean, non-broker time actually constitutes the 
majority of their stock research time.  
Yahoo Finance is by far the most important non-broker finance research site. Its 
dominance continues to this day. Some investors are power users, but more than half consult it at 
least once within the sample period. Yahoo Finance also appears at or near the top of any Google 
search involving a particular stock, which perpetuates its dominance and explains why more than 
half of investors use it at least once. Further attesting to the importance of this domain, 
Lawrence, Ryans, Sun, and Laptev (2018) conduct a field experiment and show that earnings 
announcement articles promoted to a mere 1% of Yahoo Finance users affect abnormal returns 
and bid-ask spreads.  
 
The websites of publicly traded U.S. corporations, specifically those that can be matched 
to CRSP tickers, are another source of relevant information. Determining whether such browsing 
constitutes deliberate investment-driven research requires assumptions guided by detailed 
examination of the clickstream, since so much internet activity occurs on domains of public 
corporations that is not especially investment-relevant (e.g., shopping on amazon.com or 
reviewing bank balances at citibank.com). As described later and in the Internet Appendix, we 
impose the requirement that a visit to a corporate website qualifies as “research” only when the 
same household-investor clicks at least one link on another domain that constitutes investment-
related research on the corresponding ticker. We also impose a running constraint that company

18
website research cannot sum to more than twice the investor’s overall investment-relevant 
research on that ticker.  
 
Under these rules, more than half of individual investors in the sample consulted websites 
of publicly traded companies that were also of demonstrated research interest to them. Among 
the 20% of investors that clicked on a company domain, conducted other research on the same

B. 
Summary—Total research activity 
 
 
The data present a number of new stylized and quantitative facts about “how much” 
information enters the information set through a comprehensive set of Internet-based sources. 
With respect to total research time, our best estimates are: 
 The mean (median) investor spends around 144.2 minutes (36.4 minutes) per trade on

 The mean (median) investor spends 29.1 minutes (5.7 minutes) on stock research on the

 For the mean investor, less than half of research time is spent within the brokerage

IV. 
Research breadth: Interest by topic 
We next break down the research time totals into types of underlying information 
observed, which is perhaps the most unique opportunity of all presented by these data. We report 
time spent on specific topics normalized by the number of trades that investor carries out over 
the course of the sample. While a great deal of research establishes that individual investors are 
stimulated to trade by certain types of information, e.g., earnings announcements, there has been 

=== PDF PAGE 21 ===
 
21
little ability to document absolute interest at the investor level or per-trade level, or even relative 
interest across types of information. 
In this analysis, samples are again relevant. The full sample of 484 investors gives us the 
best estimates of total time research breakdowns across categories. The subsample of investors 
for which we are able to attach a very high fraction of the investors’ links to particular ticker 
symbols provides our best estimates of the breakdown of research activity for the specific stocks 
that are actually traded—in effect, a notion of the amount and types of firm-specific information 
that the average individual investor’s trades might “inject” into prices. 
A. 
Mixed-content pages 
A complexity to carefully address is that some pages present multiple types of 
information. For example, investors frequently access detailed quote pages, often referred to as 
“snapshots” by their broker site or Yahoo Finance, which contain a variety of information—
earnings, price charts, dividends, and so on. Only eye-tracking software would allow us to 
directly determine attention to the different content types within such pages. News articles of 
indeterminate content and time spent on message boards present similar complexities. Although 
snapshots, news, and message boards are characterizations of a dimension of research, they are 
not specific types of content. After reporting time spent on such pages, we discuss a 
methodology for attributing time to specific types of content.   
A.1. 
Snapshots 
Snapshot pages will be familiar. They often serve as starting points for deeper research 
through other links and tabs, but they also may constitute the entirety of a research session. 
Further, in some cases, investors who trade a given ticker cannot avoid some form of quote page 
based on the structure of the broker’s website, although she can choose to click past it quickly.  

=== PDF PAGE 22 ===
 
22
Brokerage and finance research domains have largely converged on a format for the 
snapshot page, with some modest differences in the mix of content displayed. Snapshots virtually 
always contain current prices, daily percentage return, open/bid/ask, today’s volume, 52-week 
range, market cap, dividend or yield, a brief earnings statistic, and a price chart with some 
default lookback period. Beyond those statistics, practices vary. For example, beta was not 
reported on Yahoo Finance’s snapshot page until years after our sample period, but it was 
reported on other sites’ snapshot pages. Some snapshot pages include current headlines or 
detailed analyst forecasts, compare today's volume to a moving average, or report other 
fundamental data. Others are sparse or provide numerical data without context.  
In Table 3, we report means and medians of snapshot research time across all investors 
(in the left columns) and the high ticker match rate subsample (in the right columns), the 
percentage of those investors spending any time on snapshots, and means and medians of 
research time conditional on the investor spending some time on snapshots.  
The mean investor spends 37 minutes per trade on snapshot pages. Nearly all investors 
(93%) spend at least some time on such a page, but the median investor spends only 6.5 minutes 
per trade. The mean investor in the high ticker match rate subsample shows roughly similar 
behavior. Since investors may click on several stocks’ snapshots around any given trade, the 
amount of time spent on the snapshot of a stock for which we actually observe a trade in the 
sample is lower. Referencing the last columns, our best estimate is that the mean (median) 
investor spends 11.1 minutes per trade (1.1 minutes) on snapshots of the traded stocks.  
A.2. 
News and message boards

C. 
Specific topics 
C.1. 
Risk statistics 
The standard academic advice for individual investors is to allocate a fraction of portfolio 
wealth to a diversified portfolio and the rest to a riskless asset. Each investor in our sample has 
disregarded that advice, at least to some extent, since he or she is trading individual stocks. For 
such investors, academics offer the advice to, at a minimum, be sure to distinguish alpha from 
beta. It is interesting to see the extent to which investors consult standard risk statistics, such as 
beta and volatility, or perhaps ignore academic advice here as well.  
The data suggest that very few investors dig into standard risk statistics. Only seven out 
of 484 investors demonstrated specific interest in beta or volatility over the course of months in 
the sample, and only a few drilled down into risk information on a ticker that they traded. This 
may be an undercount of interest in risk statistics, because risk statistics are, in some domains, 
also presented as line items in snapshots. Upon imputing snapshot time to specific content types 
(which may, in contrast, lead to an overcount), we estimate that the mean investor spends 1.8 of 
her 144.2 minutes per trade of total research on risk statistics; the median investor spends a 

=== PDF PAGE 25 ===
 
25
fraction of a minute on risk statistics in all stocks, and a few seconds on such statistics of the 
stocks that she actually trades.  
C.2. 
Dividends, earnings, and other fundamentals 
 
Some investors seek out hard fundamental information: dividends, earnings, valuation 
ratios, and the accounting statements and regulatory filings that underlie these figures. We find 
more interest in this type of research, especially when considered as a whole. Around 40% of 
investors seek out some sort of earnings information, with just under two minutes of research for 
the mean investor. Dividends attract at least some direct attention by 17% of investors, and those 
with that particular interest tend to spend relatively more time on research.  
 
A considerable fraction of investors—53%—explicitly observe some non-earnings, non-
dividend fundamental information. In this disparate category we group valuation ratios such as 
price to sales, annual reports, cash flow statistics, and regulatory filings. On average, we see a 
few minutes of such research per trade. 
Earnings and sometimes dividend information is provided on snapshot pages. Upon 
adding this imputed time in the manner described above, the mean investor’s time spent on 
earnings, dividends, and other fundamentals rises considerably. Median behavior changes less.  
C.3. 
Analysts  
Analyst reports and estimates are observed fairly frequently. 71% of investors consider 
this type of information in some measure over the course of the sample. The mean investor 
spends over 12 minutes per trade on analyst reports; conditional on doing any such research, the 
mean investor spends 17.5 minutes. Time on analyst reports is skewed, with the median investor 
with an interest in analyst behavior carrying out only 2.5 minutes of it per trade.  
C.4. 
Ownership

Ownership-related research includes statistics about short-selling, institutional or mutual 
fund ownership, or insider ownership or trading. This groups together market participants that 
the average individual investor might, or perhaps should, suspect to have generally better

trade. This does not necessarily indicate disinterest, since as mentioned above it would not take 
long to read the percentage of institutional ownership or short interest outstanding; so, it is more

was none on the Yahoo Finance snapshots at the time. Overall, individual investor interest in this

nominal share price while dedicated price chart pages more often show the evolution of the share

some implicit information about risk, which is useful since investors do not seek out detailed risk

theoretically and empirically and doesn’t warrant its own primary category. An example would

VI. 
Research styles: Heterogeneity in depth, breadth, and focus  
 
The summary statistics earlier showed substantial heterogeneity in the total amount of 
time that investors spend on research. Upon investigation, the higher research time mean is not 
driven by the more frequent traders. The correlation between an investor’s number of stock 
trades and the average research minutes per trade is -0.10; and the rank correlation is -0.22. 
Research per trade is even less correlated with dollars traded per trade. Hence, the heterogeneity 

=== PDF PAGE 35 ===
 
35
is important. To understand how the bulk of research maps into trading and market outcomes, we 
need to look broadly across investors.  
 
We use principal components to characterize some of the heterogeneity in research 
behavior in more detail. For this analysis, we restrict the data to research matched to tickers, and 
we create indicator variables for any investor-category that sees more than two minutes of 
research per month. (This leads to excluding the risk statistics category.) Ticker-matched 
research allows us to augment these indicator variables with features that capture the nature of 
the stocks that the particular investor focuses on. Specifically, we construct a stereotypical-stock-
type variable based on the investor’s propensity to research stocks across the characteristics 
included in Table 4. It is higher for an investor who tilts her interest toward volatile, small, 
younger, lower nominal price “speculative” stocks and lower for an investor with more interest 
in larger, older, dividend-paying “bond-like” stocks. This coarse separation is motivated by 
patterns in Baker and Wurgler (2006). 
The first principal component reported in Table 5 is the level effect. Some investors just 
do more research on any and all topics than other investors. This shows the importance of the 
skewness in overall research time that appeared in the summary statistics. The level effect 
explains about 27% of the variation.  
Whereas the depth of research is independent of a focus on speculative stocks, the second 
principal component, which explains about 9% of the variation, does relate to the types of stocks 
investigated. The second principal component distinguishes between speculative-stock investors, 
who tend to focus on faster-moving information such as news, message boards, brief summary 
statistics, and price charts, from investors who focus on less speculative stocks and tilt their 
research time toward earnings and slow-moving “hard” fundamentals. This finding relates to 

=== PDF PAGE 36 ===
 
36
some cross-stock patterns in Table 4. There, we saw that higher-risk stocks saw more price-based 
research; here, we see that this to some extent reflects the styles adopted by a subset of investors 
rather than a general tendency across all investors.  
Beyond these two components, there is a large amount of unexplained heterogeneity in 
research behavior and, hence, heterogeneity in information sets and beliefs. The results get a step 
closer to the origins of Giglio, Maggiori, Stroebel, and Utkus (2021)’s conclusion that retail 
investors’ “beliefs are mostly characterized by large and persistent individual heterogeneity” (p. 
1481). 
In summary, with respect to investor heterogeneity: 
 Some investors show more attention to information of any and all types; the skewness of 
the distribution of research time per trade is not driven by different investors paying 
attention to different particular topics.  
 Investors who research relatively speculative stocks give extra attention to fast-moving 
information. Investors who research relatively bond-like stocks focus somewhat more on 
slower-moving information. 
 
VII. 
Stock research in the context of trading 
As a final step, we study research behavior in the context of an investor’s decision to 
trade a specific stock, as opposed to their broad patterns of information gathering. While there is 
no simple way to summarize the information gathering process for all trades, in all stocks, by all 
individual investors, narrowing focus to this perspective ties a number of the previous analyses 
together in perhaps the most important context. 
A. 
Time spent in the run-up to a trade 

=== PDF PAGE 37 ===
 
37
These data capture the temporal pattern of overall research around a trade. In Figure 3, 
we focus on trades made by the 50 investors whose links in the research-related clickstream can 
be fully or almost fully associated with specific tickers, as in previous analyses that require this 
level of detail. We allocate research time in a to-be-traded ticker to the next trade in that stock; if 
no further trade occurs, we count research time as “post-trade” research. Cumulation of time for 
any given investor-trade in a specific stock starts either at the beginning of the sample, or 
immediately after a previous trade in the same stock. In cases where the investor trades multiple 
times over the course of our sample, we create an average cumulation of research time per trade 
as a summary of that investor’s approach to research efforts prior to a trade.  
The left panel on Figure 3 plots the mean research time in the ticker traded at t=0 from 
ten calendar days before the trade to two days after. Each vertical bar marks a 24-hour period, 
and the periodic flat spots arise from pauses in research activity between typical trading hours. 
Note that cumulative research at ten days prior to the trade is already different from zero, 
reflecting the cumulation starting at the beginning of the sample or after the last trade in the 
stock. Roughly half of the overall pre-trade research of 20 minutes takes place in the week prior 
to trade, and about five minutes takes place in a burst just prior to trade. For the mean investor, 
research on buys is somewhat more concentrated in the immediate run-up while research on 
sells, being more likely to include monitoring and investigation of existing positions, is slightly 
more spread out. 
The median behavior in the right panel of Figure 3 is different. It is unaffected by outliers 
generated by a few avid researchers, so it provides a more accurate characterization of a 
“typical” investor’s behavior. The main finding is that a large fraction of individual investors do 
only a few minutes of ticker-specific research, as shown previously, and much of this takes place 

=== PDF PAGE 38 ===
 
38
in the minutes immediately prior to the trade, as shown here. The median investor carries out no 
research at all until five days before a trade. The different median and mean behaviors illustrate 
in another way the heterogeneity in research time spent across investors. 
B. 
Information search patterns 
 
What topics draw investors into research, and how does the construction of an 
information set proceed toward the crucial last topics considered before a trade? The clickstream 
allows us to investigate these sequential paths. They can be quite complex within any given 
session. In addition, for any given ticker, research can take place over numerous short or long 
browsing sessions, and an investor can double back to the same underlying content topic. Sankey 
diagrams are a useful way to summarize the prominent aspects of search paths.  
B.1. 
Search paths 
 
We start by tracking information search paths forward in time unconditionally. When a 
ticker is researched in any fashion, a fused investor-ticker-session is initiated that includes all 
research on that ticker within the next ten days. If a given topic is considered multiple times, the 
first is counted. We collapse all links into topics, given our focus here on the content itself rather 
than the particular domains that investors consult.  
 
For example, if a session begins with an investor looking into a price chart of a particular 
ticker, then turns to some consideration of earnings, then turns to dividends, then comes back to 
a price chart as a last step within a ten-day window, the path would be embedded in the diagram: 
Price Chart -> Earnings -> Dividends -> END. To preserve original detail, we do not impute 
topics, i.e., we show snapshot pages as elements of the sequence.  
 
We record these paths in Figure 4. We drop all stocks that a given investor researches 
within their first 20 days in the sample to create a purer look at a fresh ten-day research window 

=== PDF PAGE 39 ===
 
39
with respect to a given ticker. Our 484 investors generate 16,930 fused investor-ticker-sessions. 
In the diagram, the thickness of paths is proportional to the number of fused sessions in which 
that path is taken. 
 
The figure shows the following interesting features. First, snapshots are the modal entry 
point into ticker-specific research, and price charts are also quite common. This is consistent 
with Table 3 showing that such pages occupy a large fraction of research time. News of 
indeterminate nature is also a common entry point. It is unique in that it appears to stimulate, or 
is at least likelier to be followed by, further investigation, consistent with prior work showing 
that news attracts investor attention. Second, most sessions terminate with only one type of 
information (which may include snapshots) considered within a ten-day window. Third, the 
number of topics consulted does not decline geometrically. Research frequently ends after a first 
topic of consideration, but searches that proceed past more than one stage often continue to flow 
to a longer, multi-topic research process. 
B.2.  
Search paths that precede a trade 
 
From the perspective of market outcomes and the information injected into market prices, 
information gathering that ends in trades is the most relevant. In Figure 5, we condition on the 
end point of a fused session being a trade and look backward. We can then inspect the sequence 
of information observed in the run-up to the trade. This sheds light on which topics were more 
likely to have “triggered” a trade and/or provided the final confirmation of beliefs.  
 
Panels A and B condition on buys and sells, respectively. An interesting and somewhat 
surprising finding is that the sequence of topics reviewed prior to the decision to buy is similar to 
the sequence prior to the decision to sell. In Figure 3, we noted that total research time and its 
accumulation is roughly similar for buys and sells, so this figure adds evidence that, typically, 

=== PDF PAGE 40 ===
 
40
buyers and sellers tend to review roughly the same sequence of research topics. Put together, 
these patterns provide new perspective on models that highlight differences of opinion and 
beliefs as a motivation to trade. Every buy must contemporaneously match to a sell, and—
abstracting from the gap-filling role of institutional investors—it hints at how two investors 
might review similar information, at similar depth and times, yet reach opposite decisions.  
 
Finally, Panels C and D separate investors into tilts toward “fast information, speculative 
stocks” and “slow information, safer stocks” based on scoring in the lowest or highest quintile in 
the second principal component in Table 5. Though coarse, this categorization illuminates further 
heterogeneity in research styles. Note that on average, the total thickness of the paths in any 
given column in Panel C is thicker than in Panel D. The interpretation is that “slow information, 
safer stocks” investors tend to consult a broader set of information topics, which by construction 
involves more fundamentals-oriented information, while those that focus on more speculative 
stocks are likelier to review only a snapshot, or a price chart and a snapshot, before a trade.  
C. 
Summary: Research around trades 
 
In summary, with respect to research around trading: 
 For the median investor, much of the time spent on research on a particular stock occurs 
in the minutes before it is traded.  
 Stock-specific information search around a trade can take many paths, but it usually does 
not include more than two or three specific topics beyond a snapshot page.  
 
VIII. Conclusions 
 
Using uniquely detailed clickstream data, we document a number of new facts about the 
research interests and information gathering patterns of individual investors that build up the 

=== PDF PAGE 41 ===
 
41
information set—providing the crucial first input to their beliefs, trades and portfolios, and the 
determinant of what information their trades inject into market prices. We present some first 
results about how much stock-specific research is behind the typical investor’s trade, which 
topics attract most attention, which stocks are most researched and toward what topic, what 
characterizes investor heterogeneity in research approaches, and when information is gathered in 
the context of a trade.  
 
These facts significantly increase our understanding of individual investor behavior and 
the information behind their activity. Of general interest is the fairly modest amount of research 
on any topic that is done by the typical individual before a trade—on the order of minutes, often 
concentrated just prior to the trade. Of interest to asset pricing and behavioral finance, individual 
investors devote more attention to price charts than other topics such as risk statistics and 
detailed fundamentals, and there is considerable heterogeneity in which stocks are most 
frequently researched and how different individuals approach research. Default presentations 
also appear to play a role. Taken together, these facts provide new color on how individual 
investors form beliefs. 
There are natural follow-up questions that can be addressed with these browser data. A 
complex but particularly important one is how an individual investor gets from what she seeks 
and observes to what she actually trades. This paper is devoted to the basic upstream information 
gathering patterns, which are already rich. But the next question is, given that an investor sees a 
particular price pattern in a chart, or certain statistics in a table, how does that drive the trading 
decision? The full trading process involves beliefs and preferences in complex ways, but we are 
exploring how browser-based research data provide insight into trading behavior. 

=== PDF PAGE 42 ===
 
42
We can also ask whether individual investors’ research behavior is associated with 
trading performance. It is an empirical question whether the average individual investor gains an 
edge based on their own minutes (or, occasionally, hours) of research. For the typical individual 
investor, is it true that “more research is needed”? If so, how much, and of what type? Are some 
individual investors better than others and what distinguishes them? Gargano and Rossi take first 
steps here using brokerage data; in work in progress, we are extending this to how individual 
investors take advantage of information of the many sorts they have at hand, some of which, as 
we show, are considered much more extensively than others.

Internet Appendix 
Here we describe key data processing steps in more detail. The starting point is 
clickstream data for a nationally representative sample of households for January, February, 
March, and June of 2007. Households are present between one and four months; the mean and 
median is three months. For each household we observe the timestamped sequence of all URLs 
(clicks/links) that they visited, active time spent at each page, and a rough categorization of the 
type of content served at the specific page. The raw data prior to any exclusions or focus on 
investing households comprises roughly two billion links.  
A stylized extract illustrates the level of detail that it may include. Table IA1 reports 
fourteen minutes of a browsing session. The session may begin at any link. This particular 
investor is a motorsports fan but soon switches to CNBC.com, where she clicks a link providing 
current data on several U.S. and international stock market indices, the US 10-year Treasury 
yield, and the USD-Euro exchange rate.  
After a minute at that page, the investor logs into a brokerage account.7 Within her online 
broker’s research pages, she seeks out the day’s most active names, which on that day included 
ImClone (IMCL). Based on our own investigation, IMCL enjoyed good news for its cancer 
drug’s prospects. Our investor then consults the quarterly earnings performance for IMCL. From 
the link, we can infer that earnings were presented for the prior three years and estimates were 
shown for the next two years. In a separate panel, the chart showed the daily volume for IMCL 
over the prior three years, as well as a 13-day moving average. Further analysis of IMCL took 
place on the highly popular Yahoo Finance website. The investor obtains a quote on symbol 
(“?s=”) IMCL and then looks up the latest analysts’ estimates (“ae”).  
 
7 The data provider scrubbed links to eliminate account numbers, personal information, email addresses, etc. 

=== PDF PAGE 59 ===
 
59
Following a check-in with race results, the investor returns to her brokerage’s page and 
takes a different path. This time, she looks for trading ideas through a stock screener, in 
particular stocks with expected EPS growth of at least fifty percent over the next fiscal year. This 
would have yielded many results, Google (GOOG) among them. A click or two later, the 
investor comes to a snapshot page, which presents many types of financial and price information 
on the given stock in an abbreviated form. After looking at a price chart, the investor enters a 
market order to buy (“ordertype=1”) Google shares. Then she logs into e-mail and proceeds with 
other activities. 
 
Rendering this raw data appropriate for our analysis requires the following ten steps.  
Step 1. Identifying investors 
We begin with a list of 11 online brokerages operating at the time of the sample and the 
associated domain names. We read through the entire dataset and create a subset of clicks on 
these brokers’ websites. Within these we flag those that contain terms suggestive of stock trades: 
we look for terms such as “trade”, “order”, “buy”, “sell”, “shares” and so on. For instance, the 
URL in row 20 of Table IA1 would be flagged for the inclusion of at least two such terms:  
https://[broker-
1].com/invest/socreateentry?ordertype="sell"&symbol=GOOG&shares=100 
Given a link that might represent a trade, we use regular expression (“regex”) matching 
to find all links on that broker’s site that follow the same syntax. We additionally use regular 
expression matching to read out the stock ticker, the trade action (buy or sell), and, if available, 
the quantity traded and the trade price. We match the traded tickers to CRSP and keep those that 
correspond to U.S. common stock and ADRs.  

=== PDF PAGE 60 ===
 
60
Having identified potential trades, we take a close look at the click sequences to confirm 
that they represent a stock trade. In many cases, a number of clicks occur in rapid succession 
pertaining to the same underling ticker. When we see a sequence of potential trade clicks relating 
to the same ticker and with the same trade action (buy or sell) with a gap of no longer than 30 
minutes, we collapse those clicks into one trade, represented in time by the last trade in the 
sequence, with the notion that roughly the same “research” is associated with trades within the 
sequence. With this procedure we end up with 2,911 total common stock and ADR trades by 484 
distinct trading households, i.e., investors. 
Step 2. Processing browsing data 
Once investors are identified, we set about documenting the stock-related research 
information they acquire before and after trading. We read through the raw data once more and 
keep all clicks associated with these 484 households, leaving around 8.5 million individual clicks 
corresponding to some 60,300 combined hours of browsing. 
We then carry out two pre-processing steps. First, we check the URLs for special 
characters and characters that are reported in Unicode values and transform them into 
corresponding characters. This makes it easier to process the URLs via regular expression 
matching. Second, we organize the URLs into groups corresponding to the host site. For 
instance, all clicks with the stems biz.yahoo.com, finance.yahoo.com, and quote.yahoo.com are 
assigned to the same group—Yahoo Finance—which is subsequently processed in one shared 
routine. The websites we process in this manner include brokerage sites and other non-broker 
finance sites such as CNBC, Marketwatch, and SEC.gov. 
Step 3. Identifying tickers and classifying content  
 
The next six steps involve reading out tickers and research categories. Our first goal is to 

=== PDF PAGE 61 ===
 
61
identify clicks associated with stock tickers. We proceed in an iterative manner, inspecting the 
URLs until we find one that represents research associated with a ticker. For instance, at Yahoo 
Finance we might come across the URL in row 10 of Table IA1:  
http://finance.yahoo.com/q/ao?s=IMCL 
and, with the help of the Wayback Machine at 
https://web.archive.org/web/20070407055358/http:/finance.yahoo.com/q/ao?s=IMCL 
we learn that the abbreviation “ao” refers to “analyst opinions”. We then use a regex pattern to 
classify all such URLs as pertaining to analyst information and associate them with the 
corresponding ticker. 
In general, in the case of non-broker finance websites such as Yahoo Finance we are 
often able to find a version of the page on the Wayback Machine, allowing us to directly verify 
the type of content. Brokerage sites, however, typically require logging in to view the research 
material, and hence are not captured by the Wayback Machine. We were in many cases able to 
find promotional material or video guides that illustrated the content on various pages, but in 
some cases, we must decide based on the URL alone. For instance, a URL might contain the 
term “analystresearch” and a ticker, in which case it’s clear that the investor was looking up 
analyst reports. In instances where we can deduce nothing about the type of content, we classify 
the content as “Indeterminate”. 
In the case of broker websites, we typically first allow the structure of the URLs to 
dictate the original classification of a given link. For instance, we might come across the URL in 
row 18 of Table IA1: 
https://[broker-1].com/stocks/snapshot/snapshot.asp?symbol=GOOG 

=== PDF PAGE 62 ===
 
62
and a regex pattern that reads out the terms separated by forward slashes identifies a slew of 
different research sites: stock news, fundamentals, ownership and so on. We continue processing 
URLs in this way until we find no more URLs pertaining to research that also contain ticker 
information. 
Step 4. Special considerations 
A number of clicks require further attention to fully extract the available information. The 
first such special case involves URLs that reference multiple tickers, such as row 3 in Table IA1. 
In such cases we create duplicates of the underlying link and associate one copy of the link to 
each of the associated tickers. We then split the time spent on the original link evenly between 
each of the newly created copies. 
Another special case involves URLs corresponding to charts. These often refer to charts 
with multiple tickers as well as multiple types of information, e.g., a chart might plot the prices 
and volume of two stocks. In such a case, we first proceed as above and split the original URL 
into copies corresponding to each of the associated tickers. These resulting URLs, in turn, are 
again split up to correspond to the different types of information. As before, we evenly split the 
time spent at the original site between all the resulting copies of the URL. Chart URLs often 
contain further information about the information the investor is seeking out, such as the 
lookback window and frequency of data plotted. We again use regular expression matching to 
extract such information. For instance, consider the URL in row 7 of Table IA1: 
https://[broker1].com/stocks/earnings/update_earningsChart.asp?showQTRChange=1&di
splay=mountain&overlay=&indicator=volume,1,13,EMA,13&duration=1095&daysforw
ard=545&year=2006 

=== PDF PAGE 63 ===
 
63
This link reveals that the investor was charting earnings over the last three years (1095 
days) and a prediction for the next one and one-half years (545 days).  
Step 5. Cleaning tickers 
Having processed the URLs and extracted ticker information, we separately process the 
ticker information using regular expression matching. We first read out any terms appended to 
the ticker, such as country indicators for stocks trading abroad, “.PK” for pink sheets, and so on. 
We then match the resulting tickers to CRSP. We inspect the unmatched tickers and, whenever 
possible, match by hand. Reasons for unmatched tickers can be caused by stocks with multiple 
share classes (such as GOOG and GOOGL), or user input errors when searching for a particular 
entity. On occasion the ticker field instead represents the investor’s input of a company name 
(“WALLMART”) which, whenever obvious, is assigned to the associated ticker. We do not 
count the time from clicks involving misspelled tickers in the research time totals. 
We separately create indicators for identifiers of indexes, mutual funds, options, and 
currencies. These tickers are often indicated by a special character on brokerage sites. For 
example, in row 3 of Table IA1, indexes are indicated by a leading period and exchange rates are 
indicated with a double dollar sign. 
Step 6. Other activity at brokerage sites 
Having exhausted clicks that contain ticker information, we classify other time spent at 
broker and finance sites. We first seek to classify research time without explicit tickers in the 
URL. We follow a process similar to the one described above and use regular expression patterns 
to read out any information pertaining to the content of the URL. 
Additionally, we classify other activity on broker sites that may reference tickers but does 
not constitute research and is not counted as research time in this paper. The two main categories 

=== PDF PAGE 64 ===
 
64
that might contain ticker-related information are “portfolio” and “trading”: clicks involving 
reviews of current investment positions, which are generally not revealed by the URLs, and 
clicks involving inputting and confirming trades. We also classify other time spent at brokerage 
sites such as time spent on logging in, time on the main homepage, and time on various types of 
non-investing activities such as educational tools, bill payments, banking, and so on. 
Step 7. Processing company website research 
As a final type of research activity, we keep track of time spent on websites of companies 
that are in the set of stocks otherwise researched by the investor. We use the COMPUSTAT 
header file to link CRSP Permno identifiers to company website URLs and manually augment 
the list of company websites for the companies in the top 100 in terms of research activity.  
The idea behind keeping track of this time, not just obviously finance research-oriented 
time, is to provide a sense of an investor’s familiarity with the products of a given company. For 
that reason, the time an investor browses the newly released first generation iPhones on 
Apple.com would count as research time, but only if they ever research AAPL on any of the 
finance sites. We additionally impose an upper bound on such research time by constraining that 
at each point it cannot exceed twice the total of other research on the same stock. This limit 
ensures that the time spent at company websites does not skew research time totals. 
Step 8. Processing non-ticker-matched research time 
As discussed above, not all research clicks reveal the underlying stock or stocks. For 
instance, consider the sequence of URLs in rows 10, 11 and 12 of Table IA1: 
http://finance.yahoo.com/q?s=IMCL 
http://biz.yahoo.com/bw/070530/000000000.html?.v=1 
http://finance.yahoo.com/q?s=IMCL 

=== PDF PAGE 65 ===
 
65
Here the investor is looking at the “snapshot” page for ImClone Corporation (IMCL), 
then reads a Business Wire press release in which the ticker is not included in the URL, and then 
returns to the IMCL snapshot page. While we don’t know the content of the press release—and 
that particular page is not available via the Wayback Machine—the press release likely pertained 
to IMCL because it was immediately preceded and succeeded by research on that ticker.  
Based on the prevalence of such instances, we seek to assign unmatched research clicks 
to tickers if the nearest ticker-matched research before and after the click in question pertains to 
the same underlying ticker. We apply this procedure only if the bracketing ticker-matched clicks 
contain exactly one ticker. 
Step 9. Constructing content categories  
After an iterative process of reviewing the processed data until we are confident that the 
URLs are properly encoded, the preceding steps lead to around 3,000 hours of total research 
time, with roughly 1,350 hours matched to an identifier, be it a ticker, a company name, or an 
index. The last two steps involve harmonizing research content categories. As described above, 
in processing the research clicks at the various domains we typically start with the content 
categorizations by the domain itself. The categories that we obtain in this way are not perfectly 
aligned across the different websites, but there are numerous broad commonalities. For instance, 
all of the brokerage and most of the other sites see many visits to a standard page best described 
as a “snapshot”: a ticker-specific summary page of different types of information that typically 
includes price quotes, a chart of recent prices, brief data on earnings and dividends, and 
potentially links to news and analyst views. Another typical page that is available is a charts page 
with prices, volume, and perhaps technical indicators such as moving averages, candlesticks, and 
so on.  

=== PDF PAGE 66 ===
 
66
Beyond these two dominant categories, there are two other types of pages that are 
prevalent across all broker and non-broker finance sites: analyst reports and fundamentals such 
as earnings and dividends. Additionally, across different sites we may see more detailed pages on 
risk metrics, technical research, ownership, or other categories as discussed in the text. For the 
less-frequently clicked categories that amount to no more than a handful of distinct clicks, we 
code the research as “Other”, indicating that we know the type of information the investor sought 
out but that there is not enough investor interest to warrant inclusion in the tables. The “Other” 
category includes disparate information such as time spent on snapshot pages where the 
underlying security is a stock market index, or on other pages pertaining to industry- or market-
wide developments. It also includes time spent on pages relating to aggregate business 
conditions, which we construct based on keywords associated with inflation, unemployment, 
GDP, and the FOMC. We additionally add time spent at the BLS and BEA websites to this 
category. By definition, these research activities are not matched to tickers.  
For pages where it is not possible to infer any aspect of the content, while the URL is 
highly suggestive that it constitutes stock research, we code it as “indeterminate.” 
Step 10. Imputing mixed-content links to content categories 
 
Finally, three types of pages either contain a mixture of content or do not indicate the 
specific category of content: snapshots, news, and message boards. In order to attempt a full 
accounting of the content of information presented to investors, we construct an alternative 
classification in which we assign research time on these pages into primary categories. 
To impute snapshot links to specific categories of content, we obtain contemporaneous 
captures of the structure of each domain’s snapshot using the Internet Archive and other sources. 
We then allocate the time spent on the domain’s snapshot pages in three different ways. In the 

=== PDF PAGE 67 ===
 
67
“equal-weighted” imputation, we divide the time spent on the snapshot page equally among the 
specific categories represented. In the “visual-weighted” imputation, we estimate the relative 
proportion of line items corresponding to each specific category represented. Finally, in the 
“interest-weighted” imputation, we allocate snapshot time in proportion to the number of 
households that expressed explicit interest in that particular category in links in our data that 
could be specifically allocated (as reported in the third column of Table 3). The third imputation 
is an appealing and objective measure, and it is what we use in the construction of relevant 
columns of Table 3, but no results in Table 3 change appreciably under the two alternative 
approaches (results available on request). 
For news links, we often cannot trace the underlying news article for further analysis 
because it was not cached in the Internet Archive. However, for two brokers we observe in the 
URL the article title and source (Reuters, Wall Street Journal, etc.), as well as any associated 
tickers. We extract the title, tickers, and the date, and match each click to an array of articles in 
the RavenPack data using the ticker and a date range of three days. We then rank this set of 
potential matches by measuring the distance between the two titles and keeping the closest, 
subject to a minimum distance requirement. The resulting dataset of matched articles allows us 
to estimate the breakdown of news article content using the detailed content classifications 
provided by RavenPack.  
We follow a similar procedure to estimate the content breakdown of stock-related 
message boards, the last type of mixed-content link that we encounter. We scrape the present-day 
Reddit r/stocks message board and for each discussion thread we match the title, “flair” (user-
supplied keywords), and original post content to our specific research content categories. We 

=== PDF PAGE 68 ===
 
68
then back out content category breakdowns by ascribing equal time to each post, dividing time 
equally across all matched categories.

are not dramatic differences based on the snapshot imputation approach, but one intuitive result 
is that the visual-weighted and interest-weighted approaches tend to assign a greater proportion 
of weight from visiting a snapshot page to price charts and technical information. This is because 
the snapshot page is often visually dominated by price information, in the case of the visual-
weighted imputation, and because investors display widespread interest in price charts when 
such activity can be directly observed, in the case of the interest-weighted imputation. Message 
boards and news, on the other hand, more often focus on earnings.
